MODULE 01

The Dawn of the Atomic Age

Every reactor on Earth today traces back to a chain of accidental discoveries, wartime urgency, and one afternoon in a squash court under a football stadium.

Teacher's Note

I want you to notice something as you read this timeline: none of these people set out to invent nuclear power. Becquerel was chasing a completely different phenomenon. Keep that in mind — it'll make Module 02 click faster once you see the physics they stumbled into.

1896
BECQUEREL

Becquerel stumbles onto radioactivity

Henri Becquerel left a uranium salt sample in a drawer with unexposed photographic plates. The plates fogged anyway — the uranium was emitting energy on its own, with no external light source. It was the first evidence that atoms weren't the indivisible, unchanging units science had assumed.

1898–1902
M. CURIE

Marie & Pierre Curie isolate the source

The Curies coined the term "radioactivity" and isolated two new elements, polonium and radium, from tonnes of pitchblende ore. Marie Curie's work proved radioactivity was a property of the atom itself, not a chemical reaction — laying the groundwork for nuclear (not chemical) physics.

1911
RUTHERFORD

Rutherford finds the nucleus

Ernest Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed that atoms are mostly empty space, with almost all their mass concentrated in a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus. This model — electrons orbiting a nucleus — is the picture that made fission conceivable: split the nucleus, release the energy binding it together.

1938
MEITNER

Meitner, Frisch, Hahn & Strassmann explain fission

Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with neutrons and found barium in the results — a much lighter element, which made no sense under existing theory. Lise Meitner and her nephew Otto Frisch, working the physics out on a walk in the snow, realized the uranium nucleus had split in two. They named it "fission," borrowing the term from biology.

1939–1945

The Manhattan Project

Fear that Nazi Germany could weaponize fission first pushed the US, UK, and Canada into a massive secret research effort. It brought together theoretical physicists, engineers, and industrial-scale production under Robert Oppenheimer's scientific leadership — and produced both the first reactors and the first nuclear weapons.

Dec 2, 1942
FERMI

Chicago Pile-1: the first controlled chain reaction

Under a disused football stadium at the University of Chicago, Enrico Fermi's team built a pile of uranium and graphite blocks by hand. By carefully withdrawing a neutron-absorbing control rod, they achieved the first self-sustaining, human-controlled nuclear chain reaction in history — proof that fission's energy could be released steadily, not just explosively.

1951–1957

Generation I: from lab to grid

EBR-I in Idaho became the first reactor to generate usable electricity in 1951. In 1954 the USSR's Obninsk plant became the first to supply a power grid. In 1957 the Shippingport Atomic Power Station in Pennsylvania became the first full-scale commercial reactor in the US — Generation I had arrived.

1960s–1990s
A REAL PLANT

Generation II: the workhorse fleet

Standardized, larger-scale PWR and BWR designs were built by the hundreds worldwide. Most reactors operating today are Generation II, refined over decades — including after the safety lessons of Three Mile Island and Chernobyl (Module 04).

2000s–Today

Generation III / III+: passive safety

Designs like the AP1000 and EPR add passive safety systems — ones that use gravity and natural convection instead of powered pumps to cool the core in an emergency, so they work even if all power is lost. These are the reactors being built new today, before Generation IV and SMRs (Module 05) take the field further.

Why this history matters

Notice the pattern: discovery → theoretical explanation → controlled demonstration → engineering at scale. That's the same arc every technology in this app follows, and it's why Chicago Pile-1 — not a power plant, just a pile of graphite and uranium in a squash court — is the true birth of the nuclear industry.

MODULE 02

The Core Mechanics

Why does splitting a heavy atom release energy at all? The answer sits in one curve — and one simple bookkeeping trick: mass turns into energy, and you can measure exactly how much.

2.1 The binding energy curve

Every nucleus is held together by the strong nuclear force, fighting against the electric repulsion of its protons. "Binding energy per nucleon" measures how tightly packed and stable a nucleus is — the higher the value, the more stable.

🤔 Before I explain — take a guess: if you split a uranium nucleus into two smaller pieces, where does the burst of energy actually come from? Is it created from nothing, or was it hiding somewhere the whole time?

It was hiding as mass. The two fragments, added together, weigh very slightly less than the original uranium nucleus did. That "missing" mass didn't vanish — Einstein's E = mc² tells us it converted directly into energy. Nothing is created from nothing; a tiny sliver of matter simply became energy.

Iron-56 sits at the peak — the most stable nucleus there is. Uranium-235, far to the right, is less tightly bound per nucleon. Split it into two mid-sized fragments closer to the peak, and the "leftover" binding energy has to go somewhere: it's released as kinetic energy, radiation, and heat. That's the entire source of fission power, expressed by Einstein's mass-energy relation — the tiny bit of mass "missing" after the split (the mass defect) reappears as energy, E = mc².

Teacher's Note

Students often mix up "moderator" and "control rod" — they sound similar but do opposite jobs. My trick: a moderator makes the reaction more effective (slows neutrons so they're captured more easily); a control rod makes it less effective (absorbs neutrons outright). One tunes the reaction up, the other dials it down.

2.2 Induced fission: U-235 and Pu-239

U-235 and Pu-239 are called "fissile" — a nucleus of either one can absorb a slow-moving neutron and become so unstable it splits almost immediately. A typical event looks like this:

U-235 + n → [U-236]* → Ba-141 + Kr-92 + 3n + energy

The unstable "compound nucleus" (U-236) wobbles like a stretching water droplet until it snaps into two smaller fragments — typically one around mass 90–100 and one around 130–145 — plus two or three new free neutrons. Those new neutrons are the key to everything in section 2.4.

2.3 Neutron moderation: why slow neutrons work better

Neutrons released by fission are "fast" — moving at roughly 7% the speed of light. At that speed, they mostly zip straight past other U-235 nuclei without being captured. Paradoxically, slow ("thermal") neutrons are far more likely to trigger the next fission.

A moderator — usually ordinary water, heavy water, or graphite — sits between fuel atoms and absorbs kinetic energy from neutrons through repeated collisions, the same way a cue ball loses speed bouncing between similarly-sized billiard balls. Slow the neutrons down, and the chain reaction becomes dramatically more efficient.

2.4 Controlled chain reactions: the idea of criticality

Each fission releases 2–3 new neutrons. If, on average, exactly one of those neutrons goes on to cause another fission, the reaction sustains itself at a constant rate — this is called criticality, often written as k = 1. If more than one causes further fission (k > 1), the reaction grows exponentially. If fewer (k < 1), it dies out.

Control rods, made of neutron-absorbing materials like boron or cadmium, are how operators hold k at exactly 1: insert them further to soak up excess neutrons and slow the reaction; withdraw them to speed it up. The takeaway

Fission works because uranium is less stable than iron, a moderator makes neutrons more effective at triggering the next split, and control rods let engineers hold the chain reaction at a steady, controlled rate — neither dying out nor running away. MODULE 03

Turning Heat into Electricity

A reactor is, at heart, a very sophisticated kettle: fission makes heat, heat makes steam, steam spins a turbine. The engineering is all about doing that safely and reliably.

A real plant: Cofrentes Nuclear Power Plant, Spain. Those cooling towers aren't part of the reactor at all — they're just releasing waste heat from the secondary loop's condenser as harmless water vapor. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

🤔 Quick prediction: if a BWR only needs one loop instead of two, why don't all reactors just use the simpler BWR design?

Simplicity comes at a cost: because BWR steam comes straight from the core, the turbine hall itself picks up some radioactivity and needs extra shielding and maintenance controls. PWRs trade a more complex build (the extra steam-generator loop) for a turbine hall that's easier and cheaper to service over the plant's lifetime — which is why PWRs remain the more common choice worldwide.

3.1 Five core components

FUEL RODS

Thin zirconium alloy tubes packed with uranium fuel pellets. This is where fission happens and heat is generated.

CONTROL RODS

Neutron-absorbing rods (boron/cadmium) that slide in and out to hold the chain reaction at criticality — see Module 02.

MODERATOR

Usually the water surrounding the fuel — slows fast neutrons so they trigger further fission more effectively.

COOLANT

Carries heat away from the core to make steam. Often the same water that acts as the moderator.

CONTAINMENT

A thick steel-and-concrete structure enclosing the reactor, the final physical barrier against any release of radioactive material.

3.2 Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR)

The most common reactor type worldwide. Its defining feature: two separate water loops that never mix.

Superheated, highly pressurized water circulates through the core (the primary loop) and transfers its heat to a separate secondary loop inside the steam generator — without the two ever mixing. Only the secondary loop's steam touches the turbine, so anything spinning the generator has never been near the reactor core.

3.3 Boiling Water Reactor (BWR)

A BWR simplifies the design: there's only one loop. Water boils directly inside the reactor core, and that steam goes straight to the turbine.

Simpler and slightly more efficient (no steam generator needed) — but because the steam driving the turbine came directly from the core, the turbine hall itself requires some radiological shielding and controls that a PWR's secondary loop doesn't need.

PWR vs. BWR, in one line

A PWR keeps radioactive water fully contained in the core building by using a heat-exchanger "middleman" loop; a BWR trades that extra complexity away by letting the core's own steam go straight to the turbine.

Teacher's Note

If your Q&A panel asks you one thing about this module, it'll probably be "what stops radioactive material from escaping?" Have your answer ready: it's not one barrier, it's several — the fuel cladding, the reactor vessel, and the containment building all have to fail together for that to happen. That's called "defense in depth," and it's worth knowing the term.

MODULE 04

Powering the World

Where fission fits into a real electricity grid, what it costs environmentally, and the two accidents every honest discussion of nuclear power has to address.

🤔 Quick prediction: which do you think has a bigger carbon footprint per unit of electricity — nuclear power, or solar panels?

Most people guess solar is lower, but on a full lifecycle basis, they're actually pretty close — and by some estimates nuclear edges slightly ahead. Solar panels require significant energy and materials to manufacture, while a nuclear plant's huge output over 40-60 years of operation spreads its construction footprint very thin. See the chart below for the fuller picture.

4.1 Baseload power

Electricity grids need a steady floor of power available at all times — "baseload" — plus flexible extra capacity for peak demand. Solar and wind are variable: their output depends on weather and time of day. Nuclear plants, by contrast, run at close to full output continuously for 12–24 month stretches between refueling, making them well suited to covering that steady floor rather than following demand swings.

4.3 Radioactive waste management

Nuclear waste is real, but its volume is often smaller than people expect: a typical reactor produces around 20–30 tonnes of used fuel per year, versus the millions of tonnes of ash and flue gas a coal plant emits.

Low-level waste (contaminated tools, protective clothing) decays to safe levels within years to decades and is handled with standard shielded disposal. High-level waste — spent fuel itself — stays radioactive for much longer, and is first cooled in on-site pools for years, then moved to dry cask storage: sealed steel-and-concrete containers designed to safely hold it for decades. The long-term goal in several countries is deep geological repositories — stable rock formations hundreds of meters underground, such as Finland's Onkalo facility — designed to isolate waste for the very long term.

4.4 Chernobyl and Fukushima: what actually happened

Containment No robust containment building — this was the single biggest design flaw Reactors had containment structures; multiple hydrogen explosions still damaged outer buildings
Key lesson Reactor designs without inherent safety margins and full containment are unacceptable — this design was never built in the West Backup safety systems must survive the worst realistic natural disaster, not just routine failures

Both accidents drove major, lasting safety reforms — from the passive-safety features in Generation III/III+ reactors (Module 01) to stronger flood and power-loss protocols worldwide. Understanding why each happened, rather than just that they happened, is what separates an informed opinion on nuclear safety from an uninformed one.

Teacher's Note

Here's my honest advice for your Q&A: don't try to argue nuclear power is risk-free — it isn't, and a panelist will see through that instantly. The stronger answer is the one you just read: explain specifically what went wrong each time, and what's different in reactors built since. Specificity is what makes an answer sound like understanding rather than a talking point.

MODULE 05

The Horizon of Nuclear Energy

The story doesn't end with the reactors running today. Two parallel movements are reshaping what nuclear power looks like next.

🤔 Quick prediction: SMRs are smaller than traditional reactors, so do you think a single SMR produces more or less total electricity than a traditional plant?

Less, per unit — a single SMR (around 300 MW) produces roughly a third of what a traditional 1,000+ MW reactor does. But that's the point: utilities can install just one or two SMRs to match smaller grids or local demand, or add more modules over time as demand grows, instead of committing billions upfront to one giant plant.

5.1 Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)

Traditional reactors are built on-site, custom, over many years — a huge upfront cost and schedule risk. SMRs flip that: they're small enough (roughly 300 MW or less, versus 1,000+ MW for a traditional plant) to be manufactured as standardized modules in a factory, then shipped and assembled on site — the same logic that made modern shipbuilding and aircraft manufacturing cheaper and faster.

NuScale

US-based design already certified by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission — modules use natural water circulation, no pumps needed for core cooling.

Rolls-Royce SMR

UK-based design aiming to reuse existing nuclear-submarine manufacturing expertise to build reactors at aircraft-factory speed.

GE Hitachi BWRX-300

A simplified 300 MW boiling water reactor design, aiming to cut construction cost and time versus traditional BWRs.

The real NuScale Power Module — the actual NRC-certified design, not a mockup. Each module is factory-built and shipped whole; a plant stacks several inside one shared pool. Diagram: Wikimedia Commons.

5.2 Generation IV concepts

Beyond making today's reactor types smaller, Generation IV designs rethink the fuel and cooling approach entirely:

Molten Salt Reactors

Fuel is dissolved directly in a liquid salt coolant rather than solid rods. If it overheats, a plug melts and the fuel drains safely into a passively cooled tank — a meltdown becomes physically difficult to sustain.

Fast Neutron Reactors

Skip the moderator entirely, using fast neutrons directly. This lets them "burn" far more of the uranium (including material current reactors treat as waste), potentially reducing long-lived waste volumes significantly.

Why "smaller and modular" is the real headline

The biggest barrier to new nuclear build historically hasn't been physics — it's cost overruns and construction delays on massive, custom, one-off projects. SMRs and Gen IV designs are, in large part, an attempt to solve an engineering-economics problem as much as a physics one.

Walk them through the physics, the engineering, and the track record — that's a stronger answer than "yes" or "no" ever could be, and it's exactly what your Q&A panel wants to see.

REFERENCE

Glossary

Every technical term used across all five modules, in one searchable place. Start typing to filter.

Radioactivity M01
The spontaneous emission of energy or particles from an unstable atomic nucleus, first observed by Henri Becquerel in 1896.
Nucleus M01
The tiny, dense, positively-charged core of an atom, discovered by Ernest Rutherford in 1911, containing nearly all of the atom's mass.
Fission M01
The splitting of a heavy atomic nucleus into two lighter nuclei, releasing energy — named by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch in 1938 after the biological term for cell division.
Chain reaction M01
A process in which one fission event releases neutrons that go on to trigger further fission events.
Chicago Pile-1 M01
The first human-made nuclear reactor, built by Enrico Fermi's team in 1942, which achieved the first controlled, self-sustaining chain reaction.
Manhattan Project M01
The secret Allied research program (1939–1945) that produced the first reactors and the first nuclear weapons.
Generation I / II / III / IV M01
The industry's shorthand for successive eras of reactor design, from the first experimental reactors (Gen I) through today's standardized fleet (Gen II), passive-safety designs (Gen III/III+), to next-generation concepts (Gen IV).
Binding energy M02
The energy that holds a nucleus together; binding energy per nucleon measures how tightly bound — and how stable — a given nucleus is.
Mass defect M02
The tiny amount of mass "missing" after fission, converted directly into energy per Einstein's E = mc².
Fissile M02
Describes a nucleus (like U-235 or Pu-239) that can absorb a slow-moving neutron and split almost immediately.
Enrichment M02
The process of increasing the proportion of fissile U-235 in uranium fuel, typically from the natural ~0.7% up to roughly 3–5% for reactor use.
Moderator M02
A material — usually water, heavy water, or graphite — that slows fast neutrons down so they're more likely to trigger further fission.
Thermal neutron M02
A neutron that has been slowed by a moderator to roughly the speed of surrounding atoms, making it far more likely to cause fission than a "fast" neutron.
Criticality (k) M02
The neutron multiplication factor, k — the average number of neutrons from one fission that go on to cause another. k = 1 is a steady, self-sustaining reaction ("critical"); k < 1 is subcritical (dying out); k > 1 is supercritical (growing).
Control rod M02
A rod of neutron-absorbing material (typically boron or cadmium) that operators insert or withdraw to hold the chain reaction at criticality.
Fuel rod M03
A thin zirconium-alloy tube packed with uranium fuel pellets — the site where fission actually happens inside the core.
Coolant M03
A fluid (usually water) that carries heat away from the core to generate steam, often doubling as the moderator.
Containment M03
The thick steel-and-concrete structure enclosing a reactor — the final physical barrier against release of radioactive material.
PWR (Pressurized Water Reactor) M03
The most common commercial reactor design worldwide, using two separate water loops — a radioactive primary loop and a clean secondary loop — that never mix.
BWR (Boiling Water Reactor) M03
A reactor design using a single water loop, where steam is generated directly in the core and sent straight to the turbine.
Steam generator M03
The heat-exchanger component in a PWR that transfers heat from the radioactive primary loop to the clean secondary loop, without letting the two mix.
Defense in depth M03
The safety principle of using multiple independent barriers — fuel cladding, reactor vessel, and containment building — so that no single failure can release radioactive material.
Baseload power M04
The steady, always-on floor of electricity demand that a grid must meet continuously, which nuclear plants are well suited to cover.
Lifecycle emissions M04
The total greenhouse gas emissions of an energy source counted across its full lifecycle — construction, fuel, operation, and decommissioning.
Low-level waste M04
Mildly contaminated material (tools, protective clothing) that decays to safe levels within years to decades.
High-level waste M04
Spent nuclear fuel, which remains radioactive for a very long time and requires cooling, then long-term secure storage.
Dry cask storage M04
Sealed steel-and-concrete containers used to safely store cooled spent fuel for decades on-site.
Geological repository M04
A permanent, deep underground disposal site — such as Finland's Onkalo facility — designed to isolate high-level waste for the very long term.
RBMK M04
The Soviet reactor design used at Chernobyl, notable for lacking a robust containment structure — a flaw never replicated in Western reactor designs.
SMR (Small Modular Reactor) M05
A reactor design of roughly 300 MW or less, small enough to be built as standardized modules in a factory and shipped to site.
Generation IV M05
A set of next-generation reactor concepts that rethink fuel and coolant entirely, including molten salt and fast neutron reactors.
Molten salt reactor M05
A Generation IV concept where fuel is dissolved in liquid salt coolant; if it overheats, a plug melts and the fuel drains passively into a safe, cooled tank.
Fast neutron reactor M05
A reactor that skips the moderator and uses fast neutrons directly, allowing it to "burn" more of the uranium fuel and potentially reduce long-lived waste.
